# *12 Angry Jurors*: Summary and Acting Tips

## Play summary

*12 Angry Jurors* is a courtroom drama about twelve jurors who must decide whether a young defendant is guilty of murder. At the start, most jurors favor a guilty verdict, but one juror asks them to slow down and examine the evidence carefully. As they debate, they revisit witness testimony, question assumptions, and reveal how personal experiences and prejudices can affect judgment. The discussion grows tense, and the jurors disagree, change their minds, and confront one another. The play explores reasonable doubt, fairness, responsibility, and the importance of listening before making a serious decision. The jurors' names and some details vary by version or adaptation.

## Memorizing lines

- **Learn the scene's purpose first.** Know what your character wants, what just happened, and what changes by the end of the scene. Meaning gives the words somewhere to go.
- **Break the script into small chunks.** Work on a few lines at a time, then connect each chunk to the next.
- **Use active recall.** Read a cue, cover your line, say it from memory, and check. Repeat the parts you miss rather than rereading everything passively.
- **Practice cue-to-line links.** Ask a partner to read the other characters, or use a recording, so you learn to respond to the cue rather than recite in isolation.
- **Say it aloud and move.** Speak the lines while standing, walking, or rehearsing the scene's simple actions; this can help connect words with physical beats.
- **Review over several days.** Short sessions spread out over time usually work better than one long cram session. Revisit difficult lines before bed and again the next day.
- **Learn the thought, not just the sound.** Identify the key idea in each line. If you lose a word, understanding the thought can help you recover naturally.

## Playing anger truthfully

You do not have to feel genuinely angry—or make yourself upset—to portray anger. Build the moment from the character's circumstances and choices:

1. **Find the reason.** What does your character care about, fear, or think is unfair? What are they trying to make someone else understand or do?
2. **Choose a playable action.** Try “challenge,” “shut down,” “defend,” or “demand an answer” instead of simply trying to “be angry.”
3. **Let the anger build.** Start with a specific trigger, then allow the reaction to grow. Anger can be quiet and controlled, sarcastic, hurt, frustrated, or explosive; shouting is only one option.
4. **Use safe, manageable physical choices.** A tighter posture, a pause, a firmer voice, or a focused look may communicate anger without forcing your body or voice. Keep movement controlled and never strike, grab, or intimidate a scene partner unless it is safely choreographed and agreed upon.
5. **Listen and react.** Let the other actor's words affect you. Real attention often makes the performance more convincing than planning a display of anger in advance.
6. **Keep the voice healthy.** Do not scream from the throat. Use a supported speaking voice, stay within your comfortable range, and ask a director or voice teacher for help if a scene calls for sustained intensity.

If anger is hard to access, that is okay: focus on the character's objective, the stakes, and the moment-to-moment reactions. You can create a truthful performance without drawing on painful personal memories or making yourself feel bad. After an intense rehearsal, take a breath, relax your jaw and shoulders, and step out of character.
